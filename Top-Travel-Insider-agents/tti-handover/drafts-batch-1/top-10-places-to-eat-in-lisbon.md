# Top 10 Places to Eat in Lisbon

Lisbon is one of Europe's best cities for eating well without spending a fortune. Its food is built on the sea and on simple, generous cooking: grilled sardines, garlicky clams, salt cod in endless variations, slow-cooked pork and, of course, the custard tart that has become a national symbol. Alongside the old family-run tascas, a new generation of chefs is reinventing Portuguese food with seasonal ingredients and creative small plates.

This guide covers 10 of the best places to eat in Lisbon, from market halls and bakeries to seafood beer halls and modern restaurants. Each comes with a practical box: how to get there, how long to allow, the best time to go and one tip. Further down you'll find the dishes to order, suggested food routes, where to stay, getting around and budget guidance.

Opening days and booking policies change, so check each venue's website or social media before you go.

## 1. Time Out Market (Mercado da Ribeira)

![Long communal tables and food counters inside Time Out Market Lisbon](photo:time-out-market-lisbon)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Time Out Market is inside the Mercado da Ribeira, Lisbon's historic market hall by Cais do Sodré. Half the building is a traditional fresh food market; the other half is a huge food hall with stalls run by some of the city's best-known chefs and restaurants, all around long communal tables.

It's a great first stop for getting a feel for Lisbon's food. You can try seafood, steak sandwiches, custard tarts, croquettes, petiscos (Portuguese small plates) and modern dishes without committing to a single restaurant. It's also handy for groups, since everyone can choose their own food.

The downside is the crowds. At peak times finding a seat can be a challenge, and some locals see it as touristy. Treat it as an introduction rather than the only place you eat.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Opposite Cais do Sodré station (metro, train and ferry).
- **How long to spend:** An hour.
- **Best time to go:** Late morning or mid-afternoon, avoiding lunch and dinner peaks.
- **Tip:** Walk through the traditional market side first in the morning, when the fish and produce stalls are busiest.

## 2. Cervejaria Ramiro

![A platter of prawns, crab and clams at a Lisbon seafood restaurant](photo:lisbon-seafood-platter)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Cervejaria Ramiro is Lisbon's most famous seafood beer hall. It has been serving shellfish for decades in a bright, noisy dining room, and it's the place to order a seafood feast: garlic prawns, clams in garlic and coriander (amêijoas à Bulhão Pato), crab, percebes (goose barnacles) and tiger prawns, all priced by weight.

The tradition is to finish with a prego, a steak sandwich in a soft roll, as "dessert". Wash it all down with cold beer.

Ramiro is popular with locals and visitors, and queues are common. Seafood is sold by weight, so keep an eye on what you're ordering if you're watching your budget.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Intendente, near the Intendente metro station.
- **How long to spend:** An hour and a half, plus any queue.
- **Best time to go:** Early lunch or late afternoon, outside the main rush.
- **Tip:** Order a few dishes to start and add more as you go; it's easy to over-order.

## 3. Pastéis de Belém

![The blue-tiled interior and counter of a historic Lisbon pastry shop](photo:pasteis-de-belem)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Pastéis de Belém has been baking custard tarts since 1837, using a recipe said to come from the monks of the nearby Jerónimos Monastery. Here they're called pastéis de Belém rather than pastéis de nata, and the recipe is famously kept secret.

The tarts come out warm, with crisp, flaky pastry and a rich custard filling, and you add cinnamon and icing sugar yourself from shakers on the table. Inside, a series of blue-tiled rooms stretches back further than you'd expect, so even when there's a queue for takeaway, there's often space to sit down.

Belém is also home to the Jerónimos Monastery, Belém Tower and the Monument to the Discoveries, so combine your visit with sightseeing.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Tram 15 or train from Cais do Sodré to Belém.
- **How long to spend:** 30 minutes.
- **Best time to go:** Morning, before the monastery queues build.
- **Tip:** Skip the takeaway queue and head inside; table service often moves faster.

## 4. Manteigaria

![A baker pulling a tray of custard tarts from the oven](photo:manteigaria-pastel-de-nata)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

If you can't get to Belém, or want a second opinion, Manteigaria is widely considered one of the best places for pastéis de nata in central Lisbon. The shop makes only custard tarts, and you can watch them being made through the glass. The pastry is especially crisp and the custard has a slightly caramelised top.

There are several branches, including one in Chiado and one inside Time Out Market, so it's easy to fit in. The tarts are best eaten standing at the counter with a bica, a short Portuguese espresso, just as the locals do.

Settling the Belém versus Manteigaria debate is a popular Lisbon pastime, so try both and decide for yourself.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** The Chiado branch is near Baixa-Chiado metro.
- **How long to spend:** 10 to 15 minutes.
- **Best time to go:** Any time; tarts come out of the oven throughout the day.
- **Tip:** Listen for the bell. It's rung when a fresh batch comes out of the oven.

## 5. Taberna da Rua das Flores

![Portuguese small plates and a glass of wine on a wooden table](photo:lisbon-petiscos)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Taberna da Rua das Flores is a small, atmospheric restaurant in Chiado that serves creative Portuguese petiscos based on traditional recipes and seasonal produce. The menu changes regularly and is often written on a chalkboard, with dishes such as fish tartare, braised pork, seasonal vegetables and unusual takes on salt cod.

The space is tiny and full of character, and the staff are happy to explain the dishes. It's a good place to try something more inventive than standard tourist menus while still feeling rooted in Lisbon's food traditions.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Rua das Flores in Chiado, near Baixa-Chiado metro.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour and a half.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch, or arrive early for dinner.
- **Tip:** Check the current booking policy; it has changed over the years, and queues are common at dinner.

## 6. Zé da Mouraria

![A large plate of grilled fish and potatoes at a traditional Portuguese tasca](photo:portuguese-tasca-lunch)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Zé da Mouraria is a classic Lisbon tasca: a simple, no-frills restaurant serving huge plates of traditional food at lunchtime. It's in Mouraria, one of the oldest and most multicultural neighbourhoods in the city, and it's popular with local workers.

Portions are famously generous, and many dishes are big enough to share between two. Expect grilled fish, salt cod and hearty meat dishes, with daily specials. The atmosphere is busy and friendly, and it's a good way to experience everyday Lisbon food.

Mouraria is the birthplace of fado, and it's worth wandering its lanes after lunch.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Mouraria, a short walk from Martim Moniz metro.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch only. Arrive early.
- **Tip:** Ask whether a dish is big enough to share before ordering one each.

## 7. O Velho Eurico

![A cosy Lisbon tasca dining room with tiled walls and wooden tables](photo:lisbon-tasca-interior)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

O Velho Eurico is a neighbourhood tasca that has been given new life by a young team who kept its traditional character while refreshing the cooking. It's near the castle, on the edge of Alfama, and its tiled walls and simple tables feel like old Lisbon.

The menu focuses on Portuguese comfort food done very well, from petiscos to slow-cooked dishes and seasonal specials. It's lively, popular with locals and a good choice for an evening meal after exploring Alfama and the castle.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On the slopes below São Jorge Castle, walkable from Baixa or Martim Moniz.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour and a half.
- **Best time to go:** Dinner; book ahead where possible.
- **Tip:** Ask the staff for the dishes of the day, which are often the highlights.

## 8. A Cevicheria

![A bowl of ceviche with red onion, chilli and corn](photo:ceviche-lisbon)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

A Cevicheria, in the elegant Príncipe Real neighbourhood, combines Peruvian ceviche with Portuguese fish and seafood. It's easy to spot thanks to the giant octopus sculpture hanging from the ceiling.

The menu focuses on fresh, zingy raw fish dishes, tiraditos and other Peruvian-inspired plates, and pisco sours from the bar. It's a lively, casual place that makes a refreshing change from heavier Portuguese food, especially on a hot day.

Príncipe Real itself is one of Lisbon's most attractive areas, with a garden square, concept stores and nearby viewpoints such as the Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Príncipe Real, uphill from Bairro Alto, or via the Glória funicular.
- **How long to spend:** An hour.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch or early evening.
- **Tip:** There's usually a queue, so add your name to the list and explore the neighbourhood while you wait.

## 9. Prado

![A plate of seasonal vegetables and fish in a bright, modern restaurant](photo:farm-to-table-lisbon)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Prado is a bright, plant-filled restaurant in Baixa that focuses on seasonal, local ingredients from small Portuguese producers. The menu changes often and is designed for sharing, with an emphasis on vegetables, fish and natural wine.

It's a good choice for a special meal that feels relaxed rather than formal. The cooking is precise and creative, but it still reflects Portuguese ingredients and flavours.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Baixa, near the cathedral, a short walk from Baixa-Chiado metro.
- **How long to spend:** Two hours.
- **Best time to go:** Dinner, booked in advance.
- **Tip:** Let the staff guide you on natural wines; the list focuses on small Portuguese producers.

## 10. Sol e Pesca

![Colourful tins of Portuguese sardines and fish stacked on shelves](photo:portuguese-tinned-fish)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Portugal takes tinned fish seriously, and Sol e Pesca is one of the best places to try it. This small bar in a former fishing tackle shop on Rua Nova do Carvalho (the "Pink Street" in Cais do Sodré) serves tins of sardines, mackerel, octopus and more, with bread, olives and a glass of wine.

It's casual, cheap and full of character, with fishing rods and nets still on display. It's a good spot for a light meal or a snack before a night out in Cais do Sodré, one of the city's liveliest nightlife areas.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Rua Nova do Carvalho, a short walk from Cais do Sodré station.
- **How long to spend:** 45 minutes to an hour.
- **Best time to go:** Early evening.
- **Tip:** Ask for a recommendation; the staff can suggest tins that suit your taste.

## Portuguese dishes to look out for

- **Pastel de nata:** the custard tart, best eaten warm with cinnamon.
- **Bacalhau:** salt cod, said to have 365 recipes, one for each day of the year. Try bacalhau à Brás, shredded with egg and potato.
- **Sardinhas assadas:** grilled sardines, especially popular in June during the Santo António festivities.
- **Amêijoas à Bulhão Pato:** clams with garlic, olive oil, coriander and lemon.
- **Bifana:** a pork sandwich in a soft roll, often eaten standing at a counter.
- **Prego:** a steak sandwich.
- **Polvo à lagareiro:** roast octopus with potatoes and olive oil.
- **Ginjinha:** a sour cherry liqueur served in small shots at stands around Rossio.
- **Vinho verde:** a light, young wine from northern Portugal.

## Suggested food routes for 1, 2 or 3 days

**One day:** Start with a pastel de nata at Manteigaria, then explore Time Out Market for lunch. In the evening, have tinned fish at Sol e Pesca followed by a seafood dinner at Cervejaria Ramiro.

**Two days:** Spend a morning in Belém for pastéis de Belém, then lunch in Mouraria at Zé da Mouraria. Have dinner at O Velho Eurico after watching sunset from a viewpoint near the castle.

**Three days:** Add Príncipe Real for ceviche at A Cevicheria and a special dinner at Prado or Taberna da Rua das Flores.

## Where to stay for food lovers

- **Chiado and Baixa:** central, with easy access to most of the places in this guide.
- **Cais do Sodré:** close to Time Out Market and the nightlife on Pink Street.
- **Príncipe Real:** stylish and quieter, with great restaurants and bars.
- **Alfama and Mouraria:** traditional and atmospheric, with tascas and fado houses, though hilly.

## Getting around

Lisbon is hilly, so wear comfortable shoes. The metro is efficient for longer distances, and historic trams, especially tram 28, are fun though often crowded. Trams and trains to Belém leave from the Cais do Sodré area. Funiculars and the Santa Justa Lift help with the steepest climbs, and ride-hailing apps are widely used.

## Booking, tipping and eating habits

- **Booking:** Book ahead for popular modern restaurants and weekend dinners. Many tascas don't take bookings, so arrive early.
- **Couvert:** Bread, olives and other starters placed on the table are usually charged if you eat them. You can send them back if you don't want them.
- **Tipping:** Tipping is appreciated but not obligatory. Rounding up or leaving a little extra for good service is common.
- **Meal times:** Lunch is usually from around 12.30pm and dinner from around 7.30pm, with many locals eating later.

## Budget guidance

- **Cheap eats:** custard tarts, bifanas, tinned fish, market food and lunchtime pratos do dia (dish of the day) at tascas.
- **Mid-range:** seafood beer halls, modern petiscos and sit-down neighbourhood restaurants.
- **Special occasion:** seasonal tasting menus and Michelin-starred restaurants.

Lunch menus are often the best value, and many local restaurants offer a set menu with a main course, drink and coffee at lunchtime.

## Frequently asked questions

### What food is Lisbon famous for?

Lisbon is famous for pastéis de nata (custard tarts), bacalhau (salt cod), grilled sardines, seafood such as clams and prawns, and pork sandwiches called bifanas.

### Where can you get the best pastel de nata in Lisbon?

The two most famous places are Pastéis de Belém, which has been baking them since 1837, and Manteigaria, which has several branches in central Lisbon. Try both and decide for yourself.

### Is Time Out Market worth it?

Yes, as an introduction to Lisbon's food. It's convenient and has stalls from well-known chefs, but it gets very busy, so go outside peak times and eat at local restaurants too.

### Do you need to book restaurants in Lisbon?

For popular modern restaurants and weekend dinners, yes. Traditional tascas often don't take bookings, so arrive early, especially at lunchtime.

### Is eating out in Lisbon cheap?

Lisbon is good value compared with many Western European capitals, especially at lunch. Prices have risen in recent years, but tascas, bakeries and markets remain affordable.

### What is a tasca?

A tasca is a small, traditional Portuguese restaurant, usually family-run, serving simple home-style food at reasonable prices. They're often busiest at lunchtime.
