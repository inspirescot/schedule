# Top 10 Places to Eat in Budapest

Budapest is a wonderful city for food lovers who like hearty, flavourful cooking. Hungarian food is built on paprika, slow-cooked meat, fresh bread and sour cream, and it's at its best in steaming bowls of goulash, rich stews and deep-fried lángos smothered in garlic and cheese. Alongside the classics, a new wave of chefs has turned Budapest into one of Central Europe's most exciting dining cities, while its grand cafés recall the city's golden age at the turn of the 20th century.

This guide covers 10 of the best places to eat in Budapest, with a practical box for each: how to get there, how long to allow, the best time to go and one tip. Further down you'll find Hungarian dishes to try, suggested food routes, where to stay, getting around and budget guidance.

Restaurants change often, so check opening days and booking policies on each venue's website before you go.

## 1. Great Market Hall (Központi Vásárcsarnok)

![The interior of Budapest's Great Market Hall with stalls and wrought iron roof](photo:great-market-hall-budapest)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

The Great Market Hall is Budapest's largest and most beautiful indoor market, housed in a 19th-century building with a colourful tiled roof and a soaring iron interior. It's a great place to see the ingredients behind Hungarian food.

On the ground floor, stalls sell paprika in every strength, strings of dried peppers, salami, sausages, cheese and fresh produce. Upstairs, food counters serve lángos, stuffed cabbage, sausages and other Hungarian dishes to eat at standing tables, along with handicrafts and souvenirs.

It's popular with tourists, so prices for souvenirs can be higher than elsewhere, but the food is good value and the building itself is worth seeing.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** At the Pest end of the Liberty Bridge, by Fővám tér metro (M4) or trams 2, 47 and 49.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour.
- **Best time to go:** Weekday mornings. It's closed on Sundays.
- **Tip:** Buy paprika here to take home; look for vacuum-packed bags, which travel better.

## 2. Karaván

![Food trucks and picnic tables at a street food court in Budapest](photo:karavan-street-food)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Karaván is an outdoor street food court in the heart of the Jewish Quarter, right next to Szimpla Kert, Budapest's most famous ruin bar. Food trucks and stalls serve everything from burgers and Mexican food to Hungarian specialities.

The highlight for many visitors is lángos, a deep-fried flatbread traditionally topped with garlic sauce, sour cream and grated cheese. It's simple, filling and perfect after an evening in the ruin bars. You'll also find chimney cake (kürtőskalács), a spiral of sweet dough baked on a spit and coated in sugar or cinnamon.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Kazinczy utca in the Jewish Quarter (District VII), a short walk from Astoria or Deák Ferenc tér.
- **How long to spend:** 30 to 45 minutes.
- **Best time to go:** Evening, before or after visiting the ruin bars.
- **Tip:** Share a lángos with a friend; they're bigger and richer than they look.

## 3. Gettó Gulyás

![A bowl of goulash with bread on a rustic wooden table](photo:goulash-budapest)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Gettó Gulyás is a small, popular restaurant in the Jewish Quarter that focuses on Hungarian classics done well. As the name suggests, goulash is the star, served as a soup rather than a thick stew, the way Hungarians eat it.

The menu also features other comforting dishes, such as paprika chicken (csirkepaprikás) with dumplings, beef stew (pörkölt) and stuffed cabbage. The setting is cosy and informal, and it's a good place to try several Hungarian dishes in one sitting.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In the Jewish Quarter, walkable from Astoria and Deák Ferenc tér.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour.
- **Best time to go:** Dinner; book ahead for evenings.
- **Tip:** Order goulash soup to start and paprika chicken as a main to compare two Hungarian classics.

## 4. Frici Papa

![Plates of Hungarian stew and nokedli dumplings in a casual restaurant](photo:frici-papa-hungarian-food)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Frici Papa is a no-frills restaurant serving traditional Hungarian food at very reasonable prices. It's popular with students and locals as well as visitors, and it's a reliable place for a filling meal without a big bill.

The menu is long and covers all the classics: goulash, paprika chicken, schnitzel, stuffed peppers, fried cheese and pasta with cottage cheese and bacon (túrós csusza). Portions are generous and the atmosphere is busy and unpretentious.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Király utca in District VI, near Oktogon and the Jewish Quarter.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch or early dinner.
- **Tip:** Ask about the daily menu, which is often the best value.

## 5. Hungarikum Bisztró

![A traditional Hungarian bistro dining room with checked tablecloths](photo:hungarian-bistro)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Hungarikum Bisztró is a small, family-run restaurant near the Parliament building that serves home-style Hungarian cooking. It's warm and welcoming, with a menu of dishes that taste like they come from a Hungarian grandmother's kitchen.

Expect goulash, paprika chicken, stuffed cabbage, chicken soup and desserts such as somlói galuska, a layered sponge cake with chocolate sauce and cream. The small size means it fills up quickly, so it's worth planning ahead.

It's a good choice after a visit to the Parliament or a walk along the Danube.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Lipótváros (District V), a short walk from the Parliament and Kossuth Lajos tér.
- **How long to spend:** An hour to an hour and a half.
- **Best time to go:** Dinner, booked in advance.
- **Tip:** Save room for somlói galuska, a classic Hungarian dessert.

## 6. Mazel Tov

![A leafy courtyard restaurant with string lights in a Budapest ruin building](photo:mazel-tov-budapest)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Mazel Tov is set in a beautifully restored ruin building in the Jewish Quarter, with a leafy, light-filled courtyard full of plants and string lights. It serves Middle Eastern and Israeli-inspired food, such as hummus, shakshuka, grilled meats and shawarma, ideal for sharing.

The atmosphere is one of the best in the city, especially in the evening when the courtyard glows. It's popular, so book ahead or arrive early, particularly at weekends.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Akácfa utca in the Jewish Quarter.
- **How long to spend:** An hour and a half.
- **Best time to go:** Dinner, or weekend brunch.
- **Tip:** Order several mezze-style dishes to share across the table.

## 7. Bors GasztroBár

![A bowl of soup and a toasted baguette at a casual counter](photo:soup-and-baguette)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Bors GasztroBár is a tiny, quirky counter in the Jewish Quarter famous for creative soups and toasted baguettes. The menu changes daily and is written on a board, with inventive flavour combinations and some unusual names.

There's very little seating, so most people eat standing up or take their food away. It's quick, cheap and very good, which makes it a favourite for lunch or a late snack.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Kazinczy utca, close to Karaván and Szimpla Kert.
- **How long to spend:** 20 to 30 minutes.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch or early evening; expect a queue.
- **Tip:** Try a soup and a baguette together; the combination is what Bors is known for.

## 8. Borkonyha

![A glass of Hungarian red wine and a plated dish in a modern restaurant](photo:borkonyha-wine)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Borkonyha, whose name means "wine kitchen", is one of Budapest's most celebrated restaurants for modern Hungarian food and wine. The menu changes regularly and uses seasonal Hungarian ingredients in refined, creative dishes, and the wine list is a showcase of Hungarian producers, with many available by the glass.

It's a good place to discover Hungarian wines beyond the famous Tokaji, such as reds from Villány and Eger and whites from the shores of Lake Balaton.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Near St Stephen's Basilica in District V.
- **How long to spend:** Two hours.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch is often better value; book ahead for dinner.
- **Tip:** Ask the staff to pair a Hungarian wine with each course.

## 9. Stand

![An elegant plated fine dining dish on a white plate](photo:stand-budapest-fine-dining)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Stand is one of Budapest's top fine dining restaurants, known for elevating traditional Hungarian dishes with modern technique. Its chefs have reimagined classics such as fish soup, paprika chicken and goose liver in elegant tasting menus.

It's a special occasion restaurant, and it's one of the best places to see how far Hungarian cuisine has come. Book well in advance.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In District V, a short walk from the city centre.
- **How long to spend:** Two to three hours.
- **Best time to go:** Book the earliest available date as soon as possible.
- **Tip:** Check the current menu and booking terms on the restaurant's website, as these change seasonally.

## 10. New York Café or Gerbeaud

![The ornate gilded interior of a grand café in Budapest](photo:new-york-cafe-budapest)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Budapest's coffee house culture dates back to the late 19th century, when grand cafés were meeting places for writers, artists and politicians. Two of the most famous are perfect for coffee and cake.

The New York Café, inside a grand hotel, is often called one of the most beautiful cafés in the world, with gilded columns, frescoes and chandeliers. Café Gerbeaud, on Vörösmarty tér, has been serving cakes since the 19th century and is known for its pastries and its traditional Gerbeaud slice, layered with walnuts, apricot jam and chocolate.

Both are popular with visitors and prices reflect the setting, but they're an essential Budapest experience. Try a Dobos torte, a sponge cake layered with chocolate buttercream and topped with caramel.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** New York Café is on Erzsébet körút; Gerbeaud is on Vörösmarty tér, at the end of the M1 metro line.
- **How long to spend:** 45 minutes to an hour.
- **Best time to go:** Weekday mornings or mid-afternoon to avoid the longest queues.
- **Tip:** At New York Café, go early to avoid the queue outside.

## Hungarian dishes to try

- **Gulyás (goulash):** a paprika-spiced beef soup with vegetables, eaten as a soup rather than a stew.
- **Pörkölt:** a thick meat stew with paprika and onions.
- **Csirkepaprikás:** paprika chicken in a creamy sauce, usually served with nokedli (small dumplings).
- **Töltött káposzta:** stuffed cabbage rolls with sour cream.
- **Halászlé:** a spicy fisherman's soup made with river fish.
- **Lángos:** deep-fried flatbread topped with garlic, sour cream and cheese.
- **Kürtőskalács:** chimney cake, a spiral of sweet dough baked over coals.
- **Dobos torta and somlói galuska:** two classic Hungarian desserts.
- **Pálinka and Unicum:** fruit brandy and a famous herbal liqueur.

## Suggested food routes for 1, 2 or 3 days

**One day:** Start with breakfast at the Great Market Hall, then explore the Jewish Quarter with lunch at Bors GasztroBár. Have dinner at Gettó Gulyás and finish with lángos at Karaván after a drink at a ruin bar.

**Two days:** Add coffee and cake at the New York Café or Gerbeaud, then dinner at Hungarikum Bisztró near the Parliament.

**Three days:** Book a special meal at Borkonyha or Stand, and spend time in Mazel Tov's courtyard for brunch or dinner.

## Where to stay for food lovers

- **Jewish Quarter (District VII):** at the heart of the food and nightlife scene, with ruin bars and street food.
- **Lipótváros (District V):** central and elegant, near the Parliament, the Basilica and the Danube.
- **Around Andrássy út (District VI):** lively and well connected, with plenty of restaurants nearby.
- **Buda Castle area:** quieter and atmospheric, but further from most restaurants.

## Getting around

Budapest has a good metro system, trams and buses. Tram 2 runs along the Danube on the Pest side and is one of the most scenic tram routes in Europe. The centre of Pest is walkable, and most of the places in this guide are close to each other.

## Booking, tipping and eating habits

- **Booking:** Book ahead for popular restaurants and fine dining, especially at weekends.
- **Service charge:** Many restaurants add a service charge to the bill, often around 10 to 15 percent. Check your bill before adding a tip.
- **Tipping:** If service isn't included, a tip of around 10 percent is customary. Tell the server the total you want to pay rather than leaving cash on the table.
- **Currency:** Hungary uses the forint. Cards are widely accepted, but carry some cash for markets and small stalls.

## Budget guidance

- **Cheap eats:** market food, lángos, soups, street food and daily lunch menus.
- **Mid-range:** traditional Hungarian restaurants, ruin-garden restaurants and grand cafés.
- **Special occasion:** modern Hungarian and fine dining restaurants with wine pairings.

Lunch menus (napi menü) offered by many restaurants on weekdays are some of the best value meals in the city.

## Frequently asked questions

### What food is Budapest famous for?

Budapest is famous for goulash, paprika chicken, stuffed cabbage, lángos and chimney cake, along with its grand cafés and cakes such as Dobos torte.

### Is eating out in Budapest cheap?

Budapest is good value compared with Western European capitals, especially at markets, street food stalls and with weekday lunch menus. Prices have risen in recent years, particularly in tourist areas.

### Do you tip in Budapest restaurants?

Check whether a service charge has been added. If not, a tip of around 10 percent is customary for good service.

### What is lángos?

Lángos is a deep-fried flatbread, traditionally topped with garlic sauce, sour cream and grated cheese. It's a popular street food, sold at markets and food courts.

### Is the Great Market Hall worth visiting?

Yes. It's Budapest's largest market and a beautiful building, and it's a great place to buy paprika and try Hungarian street food upstairs. Note that it's closed on Sundays.

### Where to eat goulash in Budapest?

Gettó Gulyás, Frici Papa and Hungarikum Bisztró all serve traditional goulash. Remember that in Hungary, goulash is a soup rather than a thick stew.
