# Top 10 Places to Eat in Copenhagen

Copenhagen changed how the world thinks about Nordic food. Over the past two decades the city has gone from herring and rye bread to one of the most exciting places to eat in Europe, with world-famous tasting menus, bakeries worth queuing for and street food halls on the harbour. The good news for visitors is that the old classics never went away. You can still sit down to a plate of smørrebrød in a basement lunch room that has been serving it for generations.

This guide covers 10 of the best places to eat in Copenhagen across every budget, from market stalls to a dinner you'll plan your trip around. Each entry has a practical box with how to get there, how long to allow, the best time to go and one useful tip. Further down you'll find the dishes to look out for, suggested food routes, where to stay, getting around and budget guidance.

Restaurants change quickly in Copenhagen, so check opening days and booking rules on each venue's own website before you go.

## 1. Torvehallerne

![Food stalls and shoppers inside the Torvehallerne market halls in Copenhagen](photo:torvehallerne)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Torvehallerne is the easiest introduction to eating in Copenhagen. Two glass market halls next to Nørreport station hold dozens of stalls selling everything from smørrebrød and fresh fish to coffee, chocolate, cheese and pastries, with an outdoor fruit and flower market in between.

It works for almost any meal. Come in the morning for coffee and a cardamom bun, at lunch for an open sandwich or a bowl of something hot, or late afternoon for a glass of wine and a few snacks. Because everything is under one roof, it's also the best place to try several Danish specialities without committing to a full restaurant meal.

The halls get crowded at weekend lunchtimes, when locals do their food shopping, so grab seats where you can and be ready to share a table.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Next to Nørreport station, served by the Metro, S-trains and buses.
- **How long to spend:** 45 minutes to an hour and a half.
- **Best time to go:** Weekday mornings or mid-afternoon, when it's calmer.
- **Tip:** Walk the whole of both halls once before you buy anything. The best stalls aren't always the ones by the entrance.

## 2. Reffen

![Outdoor street food stalls and picnic tables at Reffen on the Copenhagen harbour](photo:reffen-copenhagen)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Reffen is Copenhagen's big street food market, set in an old industrial area on the island of Refshaleøen. Dozens of stalls in shipping containers serve food from around the world, from Danish classics to dumplings, tacos, curries and grilled fish, alongside bars and plenty of outdoor seating facing the water.

The atmosphere is the main draw. On a warm evening, with the sun setting over the harbour and music playing, it's one of the most enjoyable places in the city to eat. It's also great for groups that can't agree on what they want, since everyone can pick their own stall.

Reffen is mainly an outdoor, seasonal market, and it's at its best between spring and early autumn. Check its website for the current season and opening days before making the trip out.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** By harbour bus (the yellow boats that are part of the public transport network), by bike or by bus to Refshaleøen.
- **How long to spend:** Two hours or more, especially in the evening.
- **Best time to go:** Early evening on a dry, warm day.
- **Tip:** Take the harbour bus at least one way. It's a cheap and scenic way to see the waterfront.

## 3. Schønnemann

![A table set with traditional Danish smørrebrød and a glass of aquavit](photo:smorrebrod-schonnemann)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

If you want to try smørrebrød the traditional way, Schønnemann is the place most locals will point you to. This basement lunch restaurant has been serving open sandwiches since the 19th century, and stepping inside feels like a trip back in time, with low ceilings, wood panelling and white tablecloths.

The menu is long, with dozens of toppings on rye bread: pickled herring in several styles, fried plaice, roast beef with remoulade, egg and shrimp, and liver pâté with bacon. The tradition is to start with fish and move on to meat, ordering two or three pieces per person. Many diners pair them with cold beer and a small glass of aquavit.

Schønnemann is a lunch restaurant and it is very popular, so plan ahead.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In the city centre (Indre By), a short walk from Kongens Nytorv or Nørreport.
- **How long to spend:** About an hour and a half.
- **Best time to go:** Lunchtime only. Arrive at opening if you haven't booked.
- **Tip:** Book ahead, ideally several days in advance, and ask the staff which herring to start with.

## 4. Aamanns

![A modern smørrebrød open sandwich with rye bread and colourful toppings](photo:aamanns-smorrebrod)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Aamanns helped bring smørrebrød back into fashion with a modern, ingredient-led approach. The rye bread is baked in-house, the toppings are seasonal, and the presentation is neat without being fussy. It's a good choice if Schønnemann feels too old-school or if you want to see how younger Danish chefs approach the classic open sandwich.

Expect familiar combinations such as herring, potato, egg and fish, but with brighter flavours and more vegetables. Smaller pieces mean you can try several without feeling too full, and the takeaway counter is handy for a picnic in a nearby park.

Aamanns has more than one location in the city, so check which suits your plans and whether it's open for lunch, dinner or both.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** The original restaurant is in Østerbro, near Kastellet and Rosenborg; check the website for other branches.
- **How long to spend:** An hour.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch.
- **Tip:** Order a mix of fish, meat and vegetable pieces to share so you can compare.

## 5. Hart Bageri

![A tray of cardamom buns and pastries at a Copenhagen bakery](photo:copenhagen-bakery-cardamom-bun)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Copenhagen takes its bakeries very seriously, and Hart Bageri is one of the city's most celebrated. It was founded by a baker who previously ran the bakery side at one of Copenhagen's most famous restaurants, and the focus is on sourdough loaves, laminated pastries and cardamom and cinnamon buns.

The cardamom bun is the one to try. It's less sweet than many pastries abroad, with a strong spice flavour and a crisp, caramelised edge. The sourdough is excellent if you're self-catering, and seasonal pastries change throughout the year.

Hart now has several branches, so you're rarely far from one. Go early, as the most popular items can sell out.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** The original bakery is in Frederiksberg, with other branches around the city.
- **How long to spend:** 15 minutes to buy, longer if there's a queue.
- **Best time to go:** Morning, ideally before 10am.
- **Tip:** Buy an extra pastry for later. A cardamom bun is the perfect train or plane snack.

## 6. Bæst

![A wood-fired pizza with charred crust on a restaurant table](photo:wood-fired-pizza)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Bæst, in Nørrebro, is a relaxed, buzzy restaurant that makes some of the best pizza in Scandinavia. It's known for its wood-fired oven, its own charcuterie and mozzarella made with organic Danish milk. The menu combines Italian techniques with Danish ingredients, and the result is both comforting and very good.

Start with a plate of charcuterie and fresh mozzarella, then share a couple of pizzas. The setting is lively and informal, which makes it a good choice for groups and for evenings when you want a proper meal without the formality of a tasting menu.

Nørrebro itself is worth exploring before or after dinner. Jægersborggade has independent shops and cafés, and Assistens Cemetery, where Hans Christian Andersen and Søren Kierkegaard are buried, doubles as a park.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Nørrebro, reachable by Metro (Nørrebros Runddel or Nørrebro station) or a short bike ride from the centre.
- **How long to spend:** An hour and a half to two hours.
- **Best time to go:** Dinner; book for weekends.
- **Tip:** Don't skip the charcuterie. It's made in-house and is as much a reason to come as the pizza.

## 7. Kødbyens Fiskebar

![A plate of oysters and seafood with a glass of white wine](photo:copenhagen-seafood-oysters)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

The Meatpacking District, or Kødbyen, is a former industrial area of white-tiled buildings that is now full of restaurants, bars and galleries. Kødbyens Fiskebar is one of its anchors: a seafood restaurant in a converted butcher's space with a long bar, a large aquarium and a menu built around Nordic fish and shellfish.

Oysters, mussels and raw dishes are a good place to start, followed by whatever fish is in season. There's a strong natural wine list, and the bar is a good spot if you'd rather snack than have a full dinner.

The surrounding area is lively in the evening, so it's easy to move on afterwards for a drink.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** In Kødbyen, a short walk from Copenhagen Central Station or Dybbølsbro S-train station.
- **How long to spend:** Two hours for dinner, less at the bar.
- **Best time to go:** Early dinner, then stay in the district for drinks.
- **Tip:** If you can't get a table, ask about seats at the bar.

## 8. Hija de Sanchez

![Corn tortilla tacos topped with meat, onion and coriander](photo:tacos-copenhagen)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Hija de Sanchez is one of the best value meals in Copenhagen. It was set up by a Mexican-American chef who previously worked as pastry chef at a world-famous Copenhagen restaurant, and the tacos are made with tortillas pressed from properly nixtamalised corn. The menu is short and changes, but expect classics such as carnitas and fish tacos alongside seasonal specials.

The stands are informal and counter-service, which makes them ideal for a quick lunch. There's a stall at Torvehallerne and another in the Meatpacking District, among others.

In a city where eating out can feel expensive, a couple of tacos here is one of the best value ways to eat very well.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Inside Torvehallerne at Nørreport, or at the Kødbyen stand. Check the website for current locations.
- **How long to spend:** 20 to 30 minutes.
- **Best time to go:** Lunch, ideally slightly before or after the noon rush.
- **Tip:** Order two or three different tacos rather than doubling up on one.

## 9. Gasoline Grill

![A classic cheeseburger with melted cheese and pickles](photo:gasoline-grill-burger)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Gasoline Grill began as a tiny counter inside a working petrol station, and it built such a following that it now has several branches around the city. The formula is simple: a short menu of organic beef burgers with cheese, pickles and a soft bun, plus fries. It's the kind of place chefs go to on their day off.

It's quick, casual and consistently good, which makes it useful when you need a reliable meal between sightseeing stops. Burgers are made fresh and stocks can run out at the busier branches later in the day.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Several locations, including the original near Nørreport. Check the website for the full list.
- **How long to spend:** 20 to 30 minutes.
- **Best time to go:** Lunchtime or early evening.
- **Tip:** Go earlier rather than later; some branches close once the day's burgers have sold out.

## 10. Alchemist

![A dramatic dining room with a domed ceiling lit with projections](photo:fine-dining-copenhagen)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

For a once-in-a-lifetime dinner, Alchemist is one of the most talked-about restaurants in the world. Set in a huge former theatre workshop on Refshaleøen, it serves a very long tasting menu as an immersive performance, moving guests through different spaces, including a dining room under a domed planetarium-style ceiling. The courses are often thought-provoking and sometimes challenge diners to think about issues such as sustainability and animal welfare.

This is a special occasion splurge, and seats are limited, so tickets are released in advance and sell out quickly. If you're serious about going, plan around the reservation rather than the other way round.

If Alchemist isn't possible, Copenhagen has many other acclaimed tasting menus. Check the Michelin Guide and the restaurants' own sites for current status and booking windows.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** On Refshaleøen, by taxi, bus or bike.
- **How long to spend:** A whole evening; the meal lasts several hours.
- **Best time to go:** Book the earliest available date as soon as reservations open.
- **Tip:** Read the booking terms carefully, including any dietary restrictions the kitchen can and can't accommodate.

## Danish dishes to look out for

- **Smørrebrød:** open sandwiches on dense rye bread, the classic Danish lunch.
- **Sild:** pickled herring, served in many styles, often the first course of a smørrebrød lunch.
- **Kanelsnegl and cardamom buns:** cinnamon swirls and cardamom buns, best eaten fresh in the morning.
- **Wienerbrød:** what the rest of the world calls a Danish pastry.
- **Rød pølse:** the bright red hot dog sold from street stands (pølsevogn), usually with onions, pickles and remoulade.
- **Stjerneskud:** "shooting star", a fried and steamed fish fillet on bread with shrimp and dressing.
- **Frikadeller:** Danish meatballs.
- **Æbleskiver:** small round pancakes, mainly a Christmas treat.

## Suggested food routes for 1, 2 or 3 days

**One day:** Pastries at Hart Bageri in the morning, then Torvehallerne for grazing. Have a smørrebrød lunch at Schønnemann, then dinner at Kødbyens Fiskebar and drinks in the Meatpacking District.

**Two days:** Add Nørrebro on day two, with coffee on Jægersborggade, tacos at Hija de Sanchez for lunch and pizza at Bæst for dinner.

**Three days:** Spend day three on Refshaleøen, taking the harbour bus to Reffen in season, or plan the day around a tasting menu dinner.

## Where to stay for food lovers

- **Indre By (city centre):** walkable to Torvehallerne, Schønnemann and the main sights.
- **Vesterbro:** close to the Meatpacking District, with lots of bars and restaurants, and near the Central Station.
- **Nørrebro:** a younger, more local feel, with great cafés and casual restaurants.
- **Christianshavn:** canal-side and atmospheric, with easy harbour bus links to Refshaleøen.

## Getting around

Copenhagen is compact and one of the world's best cycling cities, so renting a bike is often the quickest way between neighbourhoods. The Metro runs frequently, and the harbour buses are part of the public transport network. Most of the city centre is walkable.

## Booking, tipping and opening hours

- **Booking:** Book ahead for popular lunch places like Schønnemann, and for dinner anywhere at weekends. Fine dining reservations often open months ahead.
- **Tipping:** Service is normally included in Danish prices, so tipping isn't expected. Leaving a little extra for good service is appreciated but optional.
- **Opening hours:** Many restaurants close one or two days a week, often Sunday or Monday, and smørrebrød places are usually lunch only.
- **Payment:** Cards are accepted almost everywhere, and some places are card-only.

## Budget guidance

- **Cheap eats:** bakeries, Torvehallerne, Reffen, tacos, burgers and hot dog stands.
- **Mid-range:** smørrebrød lunches, neighbourhood bistros, pizza at Bæst.
- **Special occasion:** seafood dinners in Kødbyen, wine bars with tasting menus and, at the top end, restaurants like Alchemist.

Eating out in Copenhagen is expensive compared with much of Europe. A good way to stretch your budget is to make lunch your main meal, since lunch menus are often better value than dinner.

## Frequently asked questions

### What food is Copenhagen famous for?

Copenhagen is famous for smørrebrød (open sandwiches on rye bread), pickled herring, Danish pastries and New Nordic cuisine, the seasonal, local style of cooking that put the city on the world's food map.

### Is eating out in Copenhagen expensive?

Yes, compared with most European cities. To keep costs down, eat at markets, bakeries and street food halls, and have your main meal at lunchtime.

### Do you need to book restaurants in Copenhagen?

For popular lunch spots, weekend dinners and anywhere well known, yes. Casual places, markets and street food halls don't take bookings.

### Do you tip in Copenhagen?

Tipping isn't expected, as service is included in prices. You can round up or leave a little extra for great service if you want to.

### Where can you eat cheaply in Copenhagen?

Torvehallerne, Reffen, hot dog stands, bakeries, taco stands and burger counters are all good value. Supermarkets and bakeries also make picnics easy.

### What is smørrebrød?

Smørrebrød is a Danish open sandwich, made on buttered rye bread with toppings such as herring, egg, shrimp, roast beef or pâté. It's traditionally eaten at lunch, starting with fish and moving to meat.
