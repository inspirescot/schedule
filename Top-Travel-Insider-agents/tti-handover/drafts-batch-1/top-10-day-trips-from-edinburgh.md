# Top 10 Day Trips from Edinburgh

Edinburgh is one of the easiest cities in Scotland to escape from for the day. Trains run from Waverley station to castles, coastal towns, golf country and the edge of the Highlands, and frequent tours cover the places that are harder to reach without a car. Within an hour or so you can be standing in a royal palace, on a windswept beach or beneath the Eildon Hills.

This guide covers the 10 best day trips from Edinburgh, with practical details for each: how to get there, how long to spend, the best time of day to go and one tip that makes the trip easier. Further down you'll find suggested plans for one, two or three days, where to stay for easy day trips, how to get around and budget guidance.

## 1. Stirling

![Stirling Castle on its rocky outcrop above the old town](photo:stirling-castle)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

If you only have time for one day trip from Edinburgh, Stirling is the safest choice. The town sits at the point where the Lowlands meet the Highlands, and for centuries whoever held Stirling held the key to Scotland. Stirling Castle, perched on a volcanic crag, was a favoured royal residence of the Stewart kings and queens, and its restored Royal Palace and Great Hall give a clear picture of Renaissance court life.

Below the castle, the Old Town is worth a slow walk. The Church of the Holy Rude, where James VI was crowned as an infant, sits a few minutes downhill, and the Old Town Jail and the narrow wynds make a good loop. On the other side of the river stands the National Wallace Monument, a Victorian tower on Abbey Craig that tells the story of William Wallace and the Battle of Stirling Bridge. The climb up its spiral staircase is steep, but the view over the Forth valley is one of the best in central Scotland.

Stirling works well for first-time visitors because it combines Scotland's two big historical stories, the Wars of Independence and the Stewart monarchy, in one compact town. Bannockburn, the site of Robert the Bruce's famous victory, is a short bus or taxi ride south if you want to add a third stop.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Direct ScotRail trains from Edinburgh Waverley take under an hour. The castle is an uphill walk of around 15 to 20 minutes from the station.
- **How long to spend:** A full day if you visit both the castle and the Wallace Monument; half a day for the castle alone.
- **Best time of day:** Arrive for castle opening time. Tour groups tend to build from late morning.
- **Tip:** Book castle tickets online in advance in summer, and do the castle first while you have the energy for the hill. The Wallace Monument is a bus or taxi ride from the town centre, not a stroll.

## 2. St Andrews

![The ruined cathedral and coastline at St Andrews, Fife](photo:st-andrews)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

St Andrews is best known as the home of golf, but you don't need to swing a club to enjoy it. This small, handsome town on the Fife coast has a ruined medieval cathedral, a clifftop castle, one of the oldest universities in the English-speaking world and two very different beaches.

Start at the cathedral ruins, once the largest church in Scotland, and climb St Rule's Tower if it's open for the view over the town and the North Sea. A short walk along the cliffs brings you to St Andrews Castle, where you can explore the underground mine and countermine dug during a 16th-century siege. From there, wander through the university quads and along North Street and Market Street, which are lined with cafés and independent shops.

West Sands, the long beach used in the opening scenes of Chariots of Fire, stretches out beside the Old Course. Even if you have no interest in golf, walking past the famous 18th green and the Swilcan Bridge is part of the St Andrews experience. Many visitors finish with ice cream from one of the town's long-standing parlours.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Take the train from Edinburgh Waverley to Leuchars (around an hour), then a local bus or taxi for the last few miles. There is no railway station in St Andrews itself.
- **How long to spend:** A full day. The journey is longer than it looks on a map.
- **Best time of day:** Late morning to late afternoon; leave time for a beach walk before you head back.
- **Tip:** Check the Leuchars bus connection before you set off and note the time of the last convenient bus back to the station.

## 3. Loch Lomond and the Trossachs

![Loch Lomond with wooded islands and hills under a cloudy sky](photo:loch-lomond)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

For many visitors, Loch Lomond is their first taste of the Highlands. It is the largest body of fresh water in Great Britain by surface area, scattered with wooded islands and backed by hills, and it forms the heart of Loch Lomond and The Trossachs National Park.

The easiest base for a day trip is Balloch at the loch's southern tip, where you can take a cruise on the loch, walk the shoreline at Balloch Castle Country Park or browse the shops at Loch Lomond Shores. Further up the western shore, the conservation village of Luss has stone cottages, a pebble beach and a pier, though it gets busy in summer.

The Trossachs, east of the loch, is a landscape of smaller lochs, forests and craggy hills often described as the Highlands in miniature. Loch Katrine, with its steamship cruises, is the highlight, but it's difficult to reach on public transport from Edinburgh in a single day. That's where a guided tour makes sense.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** By train, change at Glasgow for Balloch; allow around two hours each way. Many coach tours from Edinburgh combine Loch Lomond with the Trossachs and Stirling.
- **How long to spend:** A full day.
- **Best time of day:** Morning, before the roads and Luss car parks fill up.
- **Tip:** If you want to see Loch Katrine as well as Loch Lomond, book a tour or hire a car. Doing both by public transport in one day is not realistic.

## 4. The Falkirk Wheel and The Kelpies

![The Kelpies, two giant steel horse head sculptures near Falkirk](photo:the-kelpies)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

This is the day trip for anyone who likes big structures. The Falkirk Wheel is the world's only rotating boat lift, built to reconnect the Forth and Clyde Canal with the Union Canal. Watching it turn, lifting boats between the two canals in a single smooth movement, is surprisingly hypnotic, and you can ride it on a boat trip.

A few miles east, The Kelpies are two 30-metre steel horse heads that rise above the canal at The Helix park. They were inspired by the working horses that once pulled barges and ploughs across Scotland, and they're impressive at any time of day, especially when lit up after dark.

The two sites are not walking distance from each other, so plan on a bus or taxi between them, or visit one in the morning and the other in the afternoon. The Helix has walking and cycling paths, a play area and a café.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Trains from Edinburgh to Falkirk take under an hour; local buses link the stations to The Kelpies and the Falkirk Wheel.
- **How long to spend:** Half a day for both sites, or a relaxed full day with a boat trip.
- **Best time of day:** Late afternoon at The Kelpies for softer light and fewer people.
- **Tip:** Boat trips on the Falkirk Wheel run on a seasonal timetable, so check before you travel if riding it matters to you.

## 5. Linlithgow Palace

![The roofless ruins of Linlithgow Palace beside Linlithgow Loch](photo:linlithgow-palace)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Linlithgow is the quickest of all the day trips from Edinburgh, and one of the most rewarding. Linlithgow Palace, the birthplace of Mary Queen of Scots, is a huge roofless ruin standing beside a loch, and its scale gives you a real sense of how the Stewart court lived. It was a convenient royal stop between Edinburgh Castle and Stirling Castle, and successive kings added to it over the centuries.

Inside, you can wander through the courtyard with its ornate three-tiered fountain, climb to the upper floors and look down into the Great Hall. Historic Environment Scotland has carried out conservation work in recent years, so a few areas may be closed when you visit. Next door, St Michael's Parish Church, with its modern spire, is worth a look.

After the palace, walk the path around Linlithgow Loch, an easy circuit with plenty of birdlife. Linlithgow's high street has pubs and cafés for lunch, which makes it an easy half-day trip that you can pair with Stirling or Falkirk.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Frequent trains from Edinburgh Waverley take around 20 minutes. The palace is a short walk from the station.
- **How long to spend:** Two to three hours, or half a day with the loch walk.
- **Best time of day:** Any time; mornings are quieter.
- **Tip:** Book through the Historic Environment Scotland website and check which areas are open before you go.

## 6. North Berwick and Tantallon Castle

![Tantallon Castle on a clifftop overlooking the sea and the Bass Rock](photo:tantallon-castle)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

North Berwick is Edinburgh's favourite seaside escape. This East Lothian town has two sandy beaches, a harbour, independent shops and cafés, and views across the Firth of Forth to Fife. The Bass Rock, a towering island home to one of the world's largest northern gannet colonies, sits just offshore.

The Scottish Seabird Centre by the harbour has displays on the local birdlife and runs boat trips around the Bass Rock in season. For a short climb with a big view, walk up North Berwick Law, the cone-shaped hill behind the town; the path takes most people under an hour to the top.

A few miles east, Tantallon Castle is one of the most dramatic ruins in Scotland: a towering red sandstone curtain wall on a clifftop, with the Bass Rock behind it. Getting there takes a little more planning, but the setting makes it worthwhile.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Direct trains from Edinburgh Waverley to North Berwick take under an hour. Tantallon is a few miles further east by local bus, taxi or a coastal walk.
- **How long to spend:** A full day if you include Tantallon and a boat trip; half a day for the town and beach.
- **Best time of day:** Midday onwards in summer, when the beaches are at their best.
- **Tip:** Tantallon's opening hours are shorter in winter, so check Historic Environment Scotland before you plan the walk out.

## 7. Rosslyn Chapel

![Exterior of Rosslyn Chapel with its carved stonework in Roslin](photo:rosslyn-chapel)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Rosslyn Chapel was founded in 1446 by Sir William St Clair, and it is one of the most richly carved medieval buildings in Scotland. Almost every surface is covered in stone carvings: foliage, angels playing instruments, Biblical scenes and the famous Apprentice Pillar. Its appearance in The Da Vinci Code brought it worldwide fame, but the carvings would be remarkable without the legends.

Chapel guides give regular talks that explain the history and point out the key carvings, and these are included in your ticket. Photography is not allowed inside the chapel, so take your time and look closely. The visitor centre has a café and shop.

Behind the chapel, paths lead down into Roslin Glen, a wooded gorge along the River North Esk with the ruins of Rosslyn Castle. Combining the chapel with a walk in the glen turns a short visit into a satisfying half-day trip.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Lothian Buses service 37 from Edinburgh city centre. Check that the front of the bus says Penicuik/Deanburn via Roslin, and get off at the Original Rosslyn Hotel stop, a few minutes' walk from the chapel. There is free parking if you drive.
- **How long to spend:** Around two hours for the chapel, or half a day with Roslin Glen.
- **Best time of day:** Time your visit around one of the guides' talks.
- **Tip:** Book a timed ticket online in advance, especially in summer, and remember that the chapel is a working church that occasionally closes for services.

## 8. Culross and Dunfermline

![Cobbled streets and ochre-coloured buildings in the village of Culross](photo:culross)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Culross, on the north shore of the Firth of Forth, is one of the best-preserved 17th-century burghs in Scotland. Its cobbled streets, red pantile roofs and ochre-washed buildings look much as they did when it was a busy trading port, and the village has been used as a filming location for several period dramas.

The National Trust for Scotland looks after Culross Palace, a merchant's house with painted ceilings and a recreated garden, as well as several other buildings in the village. Walk up the hill to the ruins of Culross Abbey for views over the Forth.

Pair Culross with Dunfermline, Scotland's ancient capital. Dunfermline Abbey is the burial place of Robert the Bruce, and next to it are the ruins of a royal palace where Charles I was born. The town is also the birthplace of Andrew Carnegie, whose cottage is now a museum.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** Train from Edinburgh to Dunfermline (under an hour), then a local bus to Culross. A car makes the trip much simpler.
- **How long to spend:** A full day for both, or half a day for Culross alone.
- **Best time of day:** Late morning at Culross, then Dunfermline in the afternoon.
- **Tip:** Culross Palace opens seasonally, so check the National Trust for Scotland website before you go if seeing inside matters.

## 9. Melrose and the Scottish Borders

![The red sandstone ruins of Melrose Abbey in the Scottish Borders](photo:melrose-abbey)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

The Borders Railway, which reopened in 2015, turned the Scottish Borders into an easy day trip. The line runs from Edinburgh to Tweedbank in around 55 minutes, through Midlothian's countryside and across viaducts, and drops you close to two of the region's best sights.

Melrose Abbey, a red sandstone ruin with remarkably detailed carvings, is believed to be the burial place of Robert the Bruce's heart. The small town around it has tearooms, shops and gardens, and the three peaks of the Eildon Hills rise behind it if you fancy a proper walk.

Close to Tweedbank station is Abbotsford, the home of Sir Walter Scott. The house, with its armoury and library, and its gardens beside the River Tweed, explain why Scott is credited with shaping how the world sees Scotland.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** ScotRail's Borders Railway from Edinburgh Waverley to Tweedbank takes around 55 minutes. From Tweedbank, a riverside walk to Melrose takes around 20 to 30 minutes, or catch a local bus.
- **How long to spend:** A full day for Melrose and Abbotsford together.
- **Best time of day:** Start early if you want to add a walk in the Eildon Hills.
- **Tip:** Visit Abbotsford first, since it's next to the station, then walk along the Tweed to Melrose for the afternoon.

## 10. Glencoe

![The mountains of Glencoe in the Scottish Highlands](photo:glencoe)
*Photo: credit on Unsplash*

Glencoe is the most dramatic landscape you can reach as a day trip from Edinburgh. The glen is a deep valley flanked by towering mountains, including the distinctive pyramid of Buachaille Etive Mòr at its eastern end. It's also a place of history: in 1692, members of the MacDonald clan were killed here by government troops in the Massacre of Glencoe.

Most visitors see Glencoe on a guided coach tour, which typically passes through the Trossachs or Rannoch Moor, stops at viewpoints in the glen and often continues to Fort William or Loch Ness. These are long days, but they let you see a huge sweep of the Highlands without driving on unfamiliar roads.

If you have more time, Glencoe deserves an overnight stay so you can walk its trails. As a day trip it's a scenic overview rather than an in-depth visit, and it's worth going in with that expectation.

**Practical box**
- **How to get there:** A guided tour from Edinburgh is the most practical option. Driving yourself takes around three hours each way.
- **How long to spend:** A full day of 10 hours or more, much of it on the road.
- **Best time of day:** Tours leave early in the morning; bring snacks and layers.
- **Tip:** Choose a smaller-group tour if you can. They tend to stop more often and reach viewpoints that larger coaches can't.

## How to pick the right day trip

- **For history:** Stirling, Linlithgow Palace, Rosslyn Chapel and Culross.
- **For the coast:** North Berwick, St Andrews.
- **For Highland scenery:** Glencoe or Loch Lomond and the Trossachs.
- **For a short half-day:** Linlithgow, Rosslyn Chapel or North Berwick.
- **Without booking a tour:** Stirling, Linlithgow, North Berwick, Melrose and the Falkirk Wheel are all straightforward on public transport.

## Suggested plans for 1, 2 or 3 days of day trips

**One day:** Go to Stirling. Do the castle in the morning and the Old Town and Church of the Holy Rude in the afternoon, adding the Wallace Monument if you still have energy.

**Two days:** Spend day one in Stirling, stopping at Linlithgow on the way back since it's on the same rail line. On day two, choose between the coast at North Berwick or the Borders Railway to Melrose and Abbotsford.

**Three days:** Add a Highland tour on day three, either Loch Lomond and the Trossachs or a full Glencoe day. If you'd rather not spend so long on a coach, go to St Andrews instead.

## Tours versus going independently

Public transport from Edinburgh is good, and most of the places in this guide are easy to reach by train or bus. A tour makes sense when a destination is spread out or hard to reach without a car, which is the case for Glencoe, the Trossachs and anywhere in the Highlands. Tours also help when you want to see several places in one day. The trade-off is less time at each stop and a fixed schedule.

Going independently gives you more flexibility and usually costs less. It works best for single-destination trips like Stirling, Linlithgow, North Berwick and Melrose.

## Getting around

**Train:** ScotRail runs services from Edinburgh Waverley to almost every place in this guide. Buy tickets on the ScotRail app, online or at the station. Off-peak return tickets are often much better value than peak fares, so avoid the early morning commuter trains if you can.

**Bus:** Lothian Buses covers the city and nearby areas, including Rosslyn Chapel. Contactless payment works on many services. Longer-distance and rural buses are run by other operators, so check the timetable for your route.

**Car hire:** A car makes Culross, Tantallon, the Trossachs and Glencoe much easier. Remember that you drive on the left, rural roads can be narrow and winding, and parking in central Edinburgh is limited and expensive.

## Where to stay for easy day trips

For day trips by train, stay near Edinburgh Waverley. The **Old Town** and **New Town** are both within walking distance of the station, which saves time on early starts. **Haymarket**, the city's second station, is a quieter and often cheaper alternative that's served by many of the same trains, especially those heading west to Stirling, Linlithgow and Glasgow. Most coach tours depart from central points near Waverley or on Princes Street, so a central base also works well for tours.

## Budget guidance

- **Shoestring:** Stick to destinations you can reach by train or bus, buy off-peak returns in advance, and choose free sights such as beaches, loch walks and town centres. Picnics are easy to arrange in most of these places.
- **Mid-range:** Add paid entry to castles and palaces, a café or pub lunch, and one guided coach tour. If you plan to visit several Historic Environment Scotland sites, compare the cost of an Explorer Pass with individual tickets.
- **Comfortable:** Book small-group or private tours, add boat trips on Loch Lomond or around the Bass Rock, and consider hiring a car for the Highlands or the Fife coast.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best day trip from Edinburgh by train?

Stirling is the best all-round day trip by train. Direct services take under an hour, and the town has Stirling Castle, the Old Town and the Wallace Monument. Linlithgow, North Berwick and the Borders Railway to Melrose are also excellent and easy by train.

### Can you do Loch Ness as a day trip from Edinburgh?

Yes, but only on a long guided tour of around 12 hours, much of it on the road. Most Loch Ness tours from Edinburgh also pass through Glencoe. If you want time at the loch itself, it's better to stay overnight in Inverness or the Great Glen.

### Do you need a car for day trips from Edinburgh?

No. Most of the best day trips, including Stirling, Linlithgow, North Berwick, Melrose and Rosslyn Chapel, are easy by train or bus. A car helps for Culross, the Trossachs and Glencoe, but tours cover those too.

### Can you visit the Highlands in a day from Edinburgh?

Yes. Guided tours to Glencoe, the Trossachs or Loch Lomond show you Highland scenery in a single day. Expect a long day with a lot of time on the coach, and treat it as an introduction rather than a full visit.

### Is Stirling or St Andrews better for a day trip?

Stirling is better for castles and Scottish history, and it's quicker to reach by train. St Andrews is better if you want a seaside town with beaches, ruins and a famous golf course. If you have two days, do both.

### How many days do you need for day trips from Edinburgh?

Two or three days is enough to cover the highlights. Spend one day in Stirling and Linlithgow, one on the coast or in the Borders, and, if you have a third day, take a Highland tour.
